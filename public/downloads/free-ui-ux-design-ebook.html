Which process do you follow?

There are five stages: Discovery, Define, Ideate, Design, and Testing. Each stage has a job. I do not jump to colors before the requirements, the user, and the structure are clear.

1. Discovery

Discovery includes requirement gathering, understanding business logic, user needs, and product goals. I conduct stakeholder interviews, user research, and competitive analysis.

This stage is crucial because clear requirements build the foundation for the entire project.

2. Define

Define is the second stage. Documentation comes into the picture here. Taking everything understood in Discovery, I create documents that finalize the requirements.

Those documents include a user persona, an empathy map, and a user journey map.

3. Ideate

Ideate is where I brainstorm and generate ideas. The key activities are workshops, mind mapping, and sketching.

This stage also includes designing information architecture, flow charts, and user journey maps.

4. Design

Wireframes

Design can be wireframes or the final layout. Wireframes focus on function and placement rather than colors, images, or final styling. They show how the actual product will function.

Wireframes are the blueprints of a design. They focus on structure and layout without the distraction of color, imagery, or detailed styling.

Visual UI

Next is the UX/UI design itself: colors, typography, and icons. This is the layer that sits on the structure the wireframes already proved.

5. Testing

Testing is done with the client and the prototype. Changes are made from the feedback.

I check three kinds of consistency:

  • Visual consistency
  • Navigation consistency
  • Contextual consistency

The full UI/UX workflow

The five stages above are the process I follow. The sections below are the detailed workflow from the UI/UX Design Process Cheat Sheet: from understanding the problem and researching users to wireframing, UI design, prototyping, usability testing, iteration, and final handoff.

It is for UI/UX students, beginner designers, career switchers, and anyone who wants a clear UX design workflow. Treat each step as a prompt. Pick the activities that fit the project, time, and budget, and skip what adds no value.

The UX design process is iterative. You will often loop back to an earlier step when you learn something new.

01. Understand the problem

Good design starts with a clear problem, not a screen. Before opening Figma, answer these questions with the team or client.

Question What to capture
What problem are we solving? One or two sentences, without suggesting a solution.
Who has the problem? The specific people or user groups who experience it.
Why does the problem matter? The cost of leaving it unsolved for users and for the business.
What are the business goals? For example: more sign-ups, fewer support tickets, higher retention.
What are the user goals? What users are trying to get done, in their own words.
What are the constraints? Timeline, budget, technology, legal requirements, existing brand.
  • Problem defined
  • Users identified
  • Goals defined
  • Constraints understood

02. UX research

UX research replaces assumptions with evidence. The right method depends on the project, the time available, and the research question. There is no single correct method.

Research loop: research question → method → evidence → insight.

Example: “Why do new users abandon sign-up?” → analytics plus five user interviews → drop-off at the phone number step → users do not understand why a phone number is required.

Method What it helps you learn
User interviews Motivations, needs, context, and pain points, in depth.
Surveys Patterns and preferences across a larger group of people.
Observation What people actually do, which can differ from what they say.
Competitive research How similar products solve the problem, and where the gaps are.
Existing data analysis Analytics, support tickets, reviews, and sales feedback you already have.
Usability research Where people struggle when using an existing product or prototype.

03. Define users and needs

Turn research into a shared understanding of who you are designing for. Personas should be based on real research, not invented details.

Artefact What it captures
User personas Research-based profiles of key user types.
User needs What users require to succeed.
Goals The outcomes users want to achieve.
Pain points Frustrations and blockers they face today.
Behaviors How, when, and where they act.
User segments Groups with shared needs or behaviors.

Simple persona structure

Name and role. Example: Priya, first-year college student.

Context: where and how they use the product.

Goals: what they want to achieve.

Needs: what they need from the product to get there.

Pain points: what frustrates or blocks them today.

Behaviors: habits, tools used, frequency of use.

Key quote: a real, anonymized quote from research.

04. Define the user journey

A user journey map shows the experience from the user’s point of view across every stage, including moments outside the product.

Part of the map What to write
Journey stages High-level phases, for example discover → compare → buy → use.
User actions What the user does at each stage.
Pain points Where the experience breaks down or feels slow.
Opportunities Where design can remove friction or add value.

Simple journey structure

Columns: Discover, Consider, Decide, Use.

Rows: actions, thoughts and feelings, pain points, opportunities.

05. Information architecture

Information architecture organizes content so users can find what they need and understand where they are.

Piece What it covers
Content structure What content exists and how it groups together.
Navigation How users move between areas.
Page hierarchy Parent and child pages; the sitemap.
Categories Logical groups that match users’ mental models.
Labels Clear, familiar names for menus and sections.
Validation Card sorting or tree testing when helpful.
  • Content inventory listed
  • Pages grouped into clear categories
  • Sitemap and page hierarchy drafted
  • Navigation labels use users’ language
  • Important content reachable in a few steps
  • Structure checked with users where possible

06. User flow

A user flow maps the steps a user takes to complete one specific task. It helps you spot missing screens, dead ends, and unnecessary steps before wireframing.

Example flow: Landing page → Explore → Select → Details → Action → Confirmation.

Part What to map
Entry point Where the user starts: ad, search, home, or email.
User action What they do on each screen.
Decision point Where the path branches: yes or no, logged in or not.
Next step Where each choice leads.
Completion The moment the task is done.
Edge cases Errors, empty states, and going back.

07. Wireframing

Wireframes are simple layouts that focus on structure, not style. Start with low-fidelity wireframes — paper or grey boxes — so ideas are cheap to change.

Focus What to decide
Low-fidelity wireframes Quick sketches or boxes to explore many layout ideas fast.
Layout Where the main blocks of content and actions sit.
Content hierarchy What users should see first, second, and third.
Navigation and interactions How users move and what they can click, tap, or enter.
  • Layout defined
  • Content hierarchy clear
  • Navigation considered
  • Main user flow represented
  • Important states considered: empty, loading, error, success

08. UI design

UI design turns approved wireframes into a clear, consistent, and visually polished interface.

Element What to focus on
Visual hierarchy Size, weight, color, and spacing guide attention to what matters most.
Typography Readable body text, clear heading levels, and a limited set of text styles.
Color Consistent color roles — primary, text, background, feedback — with enough contrast.
Spacing A consistent spacing scale, for example multiples of 4 or 8px.
Grid Columns and margins that keep layouts aligned across breakpoints.
Components Reusable buttons, inputs, cards, and navigation with defined states.
Design system Shared styles, components, and rules that keep the product consistent.
  • Clear visual hierarchy on every screen
  • Text styles consistent and readable
  • Text and UI elements have enough contrast
  • Spacing follows one scale
  • Layouts align to the grid
  • Components reused, not redrawn
  • Hover, focus, disabled, and error states designed
  • Mobile and desktop layouts checked

09. Prototyping

Prototypes let people experience a design before it is built. They reveal problems early, when they are still cheap to fix, and help stakeholders understand the idea.

Prototype loop: prototype → test → observe → improve.

Type Use it for
Low-fidelity prototypes Linked sketches or wireframes to test flow and structure.
High-fidelity prototypes Close to the final UI, for testing details and interactions.
Interactive flows Clickable paths through a key task, from start to completion.
Prototype testing Put the prototype in front of users and watch what happens.

10. Usability testing

Usability testing shows whether real people can complete key tasks with the design. Even a small number of sessions can reveal the biggest issues.

Step What to do
1. Define the objective What do you need to learn? For example: “Can users find and book a session?”
2. Create tasks Realistic scenarios that do not reveal the answer or the UI labels.
3. Recruit participants People who match the target users, not only colleagues.
4. Observe behavior Watch and listen. Avoid helping or leading the participant.
5. Record findings Note where people hesitate, fail, or get confused, and what they say.
6. Identify patterns Group repeated issues across participants.
7. Prioritize issues Rank by severity and how many users were affected.
  • Testing objective written
  • Tasks and scenarios prepared
  • Prototype or product ready to test
  • Appropriate participants recruited
  • Consent and recording set up
  • Sessions observed without leading
  • Findings recorded and grouped
  • Issues prioritized for iteration

11. Iteration

UX design is iterative rather than a strictly linear process. Each round of feedback improves the design and reduces risk before development.

Iteration cycle: identify issues → prioritize → explore solutions → update the design → test again.

Fix the highest-impact issues first. Not every piece of feedback needs a change. Look for patterns and evidence.

12. Final design and handoff

A good handoff helps developers build what was designed without guesswork. Include everything they need in one organized place.

Deliverable Include
Final UI Approved screens, clearly named and organized.
Components Reusable components with all their states and variants.
Design tokens and styles Colors, typography, spacing, radius, and shadows.
Responsive states How layouts adapt across mobile, tablet, and desktop.
Interaction specifications Transitions, hover and focus, loading, and error behavior.
Prototype A linked prototype showing the intended flows.
Assets Exported icons, images, and logos in the right formats.
Developer notes Edge cases, content rules, and open questions.
  • Final screens approved and labeled
  • Components and variants documented
  • Design tokens and styles defined
  • Responsive layouts provided
  • Interaction and state specs written
  • Prototype linked
  • Assets exported
  • Developer notes and edge cases added
  • Handoff walkthrough with developers done
  • Plan for design QA during build

13. Measure and learn

After launch, check whether the design worked. The right metrics depend on the project and its goals. Choose a few that connect to the problem defined in step 01.

Signal Examples
User feedback Comments, reviews, and support messages.
Usability findings Results from follow-up testing.
Task success Can users complete key tasks?
Conversion Sign-ups, purchases, or bookings.
Engagement Return visits and feature usage.
Business and product metrics Retention, revenue, fewer support tickets.

How the workflow loops

Real projects do not always follow this process in a perfectly linear order. Designers move between research, flows, wireframes, prototypes, and testing as new information is discovered.

Common loops:

Usability testing → back to wireframes or UI design.

A new research insight → update personas, the journey, or the user flow.

Measure and learn → a new problem to solve, and the process starts again.

Quick process checklist

Run through this before sharing a project or handing it off.

  • Problem understood
  • Users identified
  • Goals defined
  • Research completed
  • Insights documented
  • User needs identified
  • Journey mapped
  • Information architecture considered
  • User flow created
  • Wireframes created
  • UI designed
  • Prototype created
  • Usability tested
  • Feedback analyzed
  • Design iterated
  • Final design reviewed
  • Handoff prepared
  • Outcomes measured where possible

Beginner tips

Don’t jump straight into visual design

Structure and flow come before colors and fonts.

Understand the problem before designing

A beautiful solution to the wrong problem still fails.

Talk to users when appropriate

Even a few conversations can change your direction.

Explain why you made design decisions

Link each decision to research, goals, or testing.

Test ideas early

Test sketches and wireframes, not only the final UI.

Iterate based on evidence

Use findings and patterns, not personal preference.

Keep users and business goals in view

Good UX serves both at the same time.

Document the process for your portfolio

Save research notes, sketches, and versions as you go.
