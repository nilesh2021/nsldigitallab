1. UX Research Planning Template

Fill this in before you talk to a single user. A one-page plan keeps the study focused and makes it easy to get buy-in from your team.

Tip: write three to five research questions. These are what you want to learn, not the questions you will ask users.

Choosing a method: interviews explain why people behave a certain way; usability tests show where they struggle; surveys tell you how common something is.

Participants: five to eight interviews per user group usually reveal the main patterns.

2. User Interview Guide

Use these user interview questions as a script. Adapt the wording to your product, skip what does not apply, and always follow up with “Why?” or “Can you tell me more?”.

Introduction (read aloud)

“Thanks for joining. I’m [name] and I’m working on [product/project]. Today I’d like to learn about how you [activity]. There are no right or wrong answers. We’re testing ideas, not you.”

“This will take about [30–45] minutes. Is it okay if I record the session for my notes? The recording will only be used by our team.”

“Please be honest. Negative feedback is just as helpful as positive feedback. Any questions before we start?”

Warm-up questions

  • Q1
    Tell me a little about yourself and what a typical weekday looks like for you.
  • Q2
    What devices do you use most during the day, and for what?

Background questions

  • Q3
    How long have you been [doing the activity, e.g. ordering food online]?
  • Q4
    How did you first start doing it, and what made you start?

Current behavior questions

  • Q5
    Walk me through the last time you [did the activity]. What happened, step by step?
  • Q6
    Which apps, tools, or websites do you use for this today?
  • Q7
    How often do you do this, and at what time of day?

Problem / pain-point questions

  • Q8
    What is the most frustrating part of [the activity]?
  • Q9
    Tell me about a time when something went wrong. What did you do next?
  • Q10
    Have you found any workarounds for those problems?
  • Q11
    Is there anything that makes you give up or put it off?

Needs and goals questions

  • Q12
    What are you trying to achieve when you [do the activity]?
  • Q13
    What does a good result look like for you?
  • Q14
    What matters most to you when choosing a [product/service]?

Product / service experience questions

  • Q15
    What do you like most about the tool you use now?
  • Q16
    What would you change about it if you could?
  • Q17
    Have you ever switched to a different product for this? What made you switch?
  • Q18
    How do you decide whether a [product/service] can be trusted?

Closing questions

  • Q19
    Is there anything about [the activity] that we didn’t talk about but you think is important?
  • Q20
    Do you know anyone else who might be happy to talk to us about this?

Follow-up prompts: “Why is that?”, “Can you show me?”, “What happened next?”, “How did that make you feel?”

Avoid: asking “Would you use a feature that…?”. People are poor at predicting their future behavior. Ask about what they did in the past instead.

3. User Interview Notes Template

Use one copy per participant. Fill it in during the session or straight after, while details are fresh.

Tip: capture exact quotes in quotation marks and your own interpretations separately. You will need the real words later as evidence.

Observation vs. insight: an observation is what happened. An insight explains why it happened and why it matters for design.

Opportunity: phrase it as “How might we…?” so it stays open to more than one solution.

6. Affinity Mapping Guide

Affinity mapping helps you find patterns across many interviews. Use sticky notes on a wall, or a whiteboard tool such as FigJam or Miro.

  • 1. Collect observationsWrite one observation, quote, or pain point per sticky note. Add the participant ID (e.g. P4) so every note can be traced back.
  • 2. Group similar observationsMove notes that feel related next to each other. Do this silently at first and do not name the groups yet.
  • 3. Identify patternsLook for groups that contain notes from several participants. One person’s comment is an anecdote; the same issue from four people is a pattern.
  • 4. Name groupsGive each group a short label that describes the user’s experience, e.g. “Unsure if payment worked” rather than “Payment”.
  • 5. Convert patterns into UX insightsFor each strong group, write one sentence explaining why it happens and what it means for the design. Move these into the findings template above.

7. User Persona Starter Template

Build personas from research findings, not guesses. Every point below should link back to something you heard or saw.

Avoid: hobbies, favorite brands, and other details that will never affect a design decision.

8. UX Research Summary Template

Share this one-page summary with your team and stakeholders when the research is done.

Tip: lead with the findings. Stakeholders rarely read the method first, so put it at the bottom if you are presenting.

9. UX Research Checklist

  • Research objective definedYou can explain in one sentence what you want to learn.
  • Target users identifiedYou know exactly who qualifies as a participant.
  • Research questions preparedThree to five questions guide the study.
  • Research method selectedThe method fits the questions (interviews, usability tests, surveys).
  • Participants identifiedSessions are scheduled and consent is arranged.
  • Interviews conductedAll sessions are complete and recordings or notes are saved.
  • Notes organizedNotes are cleaned up and labeled by participant.
  • Patterns identifiedAffinity map shows groups supported by several participants.
  • Insights documentedEach insight has evidence behind it.
  • Opportunities identifiedInsights are turned into “How might we…?” statements.
  • Findings shared with stakeholdersThe research summary has been presented or sent to the team.

10. UX Research Tips for Beginners

Avoid leading questions

Ask “How do you feel about the checkout?” instead of “Don’t you think the checkout is confusing?”.

Ask open-ended questions

Start with how, what, or tell me about. Questions that can be answered with yes or no end the conversation.

Listen more than you speak

Aim for the participant talking about 80% of the time. Get comfortable with silence, because people often fill it with the most useful detail.

Ask users about real behavior

“Tell me about the last time you…” gives better data than “What would you do if…”.

Separate observations from assumptions

Write down what happened, then add your interpretation in a separate line or color.

Look for patterns, not isolated comments

One loud opinion is not a finding. Look for the same issue across several participants.

Support important findings with evidence

Back each key finding with quotes, participant counts, or recordings so the team can trust it.

Understand the problem before designing

Resist sketching solutions during research. Collect what you learn first, then design.
