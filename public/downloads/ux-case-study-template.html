UX Case Study Template

Structure a portfolio case study from problem discovery to results. Click any field to type. Your answers are saved in this browser. Each section has short guidance on what to write, what evidence to add, and what to avoid. Beginners can keep answers short; experienced designers can go deeper on decisions and impact.

1. Project Overview

Write: the facts a recruiter needs in ten seconds: what, how long, who, and your part.

Include: a hero image or final screen mockup next to this summary.

Avoid: listing every tool you know. Name only the tools used on this project.

2. Problem Statement

Write: one or two sentences that name the user, the problem, and the cost of leaving it unsolved.

Include: a support ticket quote, analytics screenshot, or screenshot of the old experience.

Avoid: describing the solution here. Keep the problem separate from your design.

3. Project Goals

Write: one primary goal and a few secondary ones, with a way to tell whether you met them.

Include: business and user goals side by side if both matter.

Avoid: vague goals like “improve the experience” without a measure.

4. User Research

Write: why you chose each method and the three to five findings that shaped the design.

Include: affinity map photos, survey charts, or short user quotes.

Avoid: pasting raw notes or claiming research you did not run.

5. User Persona

Write: a persona based on your research, not on guesses.

Include: one clean persona card. Link pain points back to your findings.

Avoid: hobbies, stock photos, and details that do not affect design decisions.

Write: the steps your persona takes today and where they struggle.

Include: a journey map graphic with the biggest pain point highlighted.

Avoid: too many stages. Four to six is usually enough to tell the story.

7. Information Architecture

Write: how content is grouped and why, especially if card sorting or research informed it.

Include: a sitemap or IA diagram.

Avoid: an unreadable diagram. Zoom in on the parts that matter.

8. User Flow

Write: the main task path, including errors and alternative paths.

Include: a flow diagram for the most important task.

Avoid: documenting every possible path. Focus on the flow your design improves.

9. Ideation

Write: how you moved from findings to ideas and how you chose between them.

Include: sketches, Crazy 8s, or a prioritization matrix.

Avoid: showing only the winning idea. Rejected options show your judgment.

10. Wireframing

Write: the key layout decisions and the reason behind each.

Include: two or three annotated wireframes, ideally version 1 next to the later version.

Avoid: dumping every frame. Show the ones that explain a decision.

11. UI Design

Write: how visual choices support the users and the brand.

Include: a style guide board, component sheet, and accessibility contrast notes.

Avoid: listing hex codes without explaining the choices.

12. Prototype

Write: what the prototype was built to test.

Include: a Figma prototype link, a short screen recording, or GIFs of key interactions.

Avoid: broken or private links. Check access before publishing.

13. Usability Testing

Write: who tested, what they tried to do, and what happened.

Include: task success rates, time on task, quotes, or a findings table.

Avoid: saying “users loved it” without evidence.

14. Iterations

Write: each change as problem, change, and reason.

Include: before-and-after screens for the most important changes.

Avoid: cosmetic changes with no link to a finding.

15. Final Solution

Write: how each key feature solves a problem you named earlier.

Include: high-fidelity mockups in device frames and short captions.

Avoid: a long wall of screens with no explanation.

16. Results / Impact

Write: real results where you have them. For concept projects, report test results and expected impact.

Include: before-and-after metrics, charts, or testimonials.

Avoid: invented numbers or sharing confidential data without permission.

17. Learnings

Write: honest reflections that show how you think and grow.

Include: a specific example for each learning.

Avoid: generic lines like “I learned teamwork is important.”

18. Portfolio Presentation Checklist

  • Clear problem statementThe reader knows the user, the problem, and why it matters.
  • Research includedMethods and key findings are shown with evidence.
  • User needs explainedPersona or needs link directly to design decisions.
  • Design decisions explainedEach major choice has a reason.
  • Before/after where relevantRedesigns and iterations show what changed.
  • Final UI shownHigh-fidelity screens with captions.
  • Prototype includedWorking link or recording of key flows.
  • Results includedMetrics, test results, or feedback.
  • Personal contribution clearly explainedTeam work is credited and your role is specific.
